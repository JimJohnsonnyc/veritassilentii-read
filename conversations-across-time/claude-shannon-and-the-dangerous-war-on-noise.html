Conversation No. 5 · September 2026

Claude Shannon and the Dangerous War on Noise

Some of the most important signals in our lives first arrive disguised as noise.

By J. Kimball

Imagine two young mothers meeting for a morning gym class sometime in the 1960s.

They know each other well. They live in the same neighborhood. Their husbands know each other. Their children play together. Their lives overlap enough that much of what needs to be said does not actually need to be said.

The conversation might sound something like this:

A: You?
B: Ok.
A: Kids?
B: Sniffles.
A: Bob?
B: Traveling.
A: Where?
B: Head office.
A: Lunch?
B: When?
A: Noon?
B: Good.
A: Alice too?
B: Yea.
A: Great.

To someone listening in, it barely sounds like a conversation.

Whole sentences are missing. Context is assumed. Predictable words have disappeared.

Yet the two women understand each other perfectly.

Now imagine one more exchange.

A: Oh, one more thing.
B: What?
A: I'm divorcing Bob.

Three words. And suddenly, almost everything changes.

Those three words contain more meaningful new information than the entire conversation that preceded them.

Why?

Because nobody expected them.

And that brings us to one of the most consequential thinkers of the modern technological world.

Claude Shannon. The Man Who Invented Information Theory

In 1948, while working at Bell Labs, Shannon published A Mathematical Theory of Communication.

It became the foundation of what we now call information theory.

Shannon was not primarily concerned with whether a message was profound, useful, truthful, interesting, or even intelligible to a human being.

He was trying to solve an engineering problem.

How much information can be transmitted through a communications channel? How do we measure it?

And how do we distinguish the signal from the noise?

And in answering those questions, Shannon did something extraordinary. He created the science and mathematics of information.

At its simplest, the insight is almost counterintuitive:

The more predictable a message is, the less new information it contains.

If I tell you tomorrow that the sun rose this morning, I will have told you something true. But I have given you almost no new information.

You expected it.

If I tell you that the sun did not rise this morning, the informational content is enormous. Something profoundly unexpected has happened.

That is why our little telephone conversation matters.

"Bob? Traveling again." Almost completely predictable.

"Kids? Sniffles." No great surprise there either.

But: "I'm divorcing Bob." The predictable probability collapses. Uncertainty changes. Information explodes.

And more than seventy-five years later, that gives us an interesting way to think about artificial intelligence.

Because we are building the greatest prediction machines in history.

The Agentic World

Much of modern AI is built around an extraordinarily powerful capability: predicting what comes next.

What word is likely to follow this one? What product are you likely to buy? What movie will you probably enjoy? Which candidate is most likely to succeed? Which customer is likely to leave? Which route are you likely to prefer? Which person should you meet? Which opportunity fits your background?

And increasingly: what should you do next?

The better the agent understands us, the more personal it is, and the better its predictions can become.

It learns our histories and preferences…our purchases, relationships, successes, failures, and habits…and perhaps eventually our ambitions, fears, and appetite for risk.

We generally describe this as progress. And it is, most of the time. But as you read further, you may decide to change your thinking.

Let's start with the progress. Prediction removes enormous amounts of friction from life. We don't want to evaluate 5,000 restaurants before dinner. We don't want to read every available document before a meeting. And we don't want to examine every flight, every hotel, every investment, every candidate, or every possible route ourselves.

We want the machine to separate what matters from what doesn't. We want it to find the important signals.

And eliminate the noise.

But Shannon's world raises an uncomfortable question.

What If the Noise Isn't Noise at All?

Engineers have spent generations trying to eliminate noise from our communications systems…radios, TV, mobile phones. That makes perfect sense.

Noise corrupts the signal.

But applying cold analytic engineering principles to human lives may not be the best idea…after all, we are not mechanical communication devices.

And what looks like irrelevant noise in one framework can become enormously consequential in another.

Consider how many important events in life could begin with something that initially appears to have little relevance. While you might think the following list is too long, if you stop to think for a moment, it is only a small fraction of the possibilities.

  • A conversation you didn't intend to have.
  • A person you weren't supposed to meet.
  • A book unrelated to the problem you were trying to solve.
  • A job you probably shouldn't have taken.
  • A trip that went wrong.
  • A failed business.
  • An unsuccessful experiment.
  • A wrong turn.
  • An idea from an industry completely unrelated to your own.

Most of these events lead nowhere important or life-changing. That's important because most noise really is, well, just noise.

But occasionally, something strange happens. Something very different from what noise might do in an electronic circuit.

The irrelevant person introduces you to someone who changes your career. The book you picked up for no particular reason gives you the idea that becomes your business. The wrong turn becomes the right one.

Looking backward, we call these moments serendipity. But looking forward, they have a different characteristic.

They contained an explosion of unseen and unpredictable possibilities.

And that creates a problem for a world increasingly organized around prediction.

The Future We Are Most Likely to Want

Suppose your personal AI agent knows almost everything relevant about you.

It knows what you've done. What you've enjoyed. What you've rejected. What you've purchased. Who you know. Where you've traveled. What you've read. Which risks you've taken. And perhaps the behavior of millions of other people sufficiently similar to you.

Its job is to help. So, it predicts a future that makes sense given your past.

It recommends the restaurant you are most likely to enjoy. The article you're most likely to read. The person you're most likely to find useful. The investment consistent with your risk profile. The job most compatible with your career. The vacation that resembles vacations you've enjoyed before.

Every recommendation may be excellent. But something may still be wrong. Because the machine is necessarily reasoning from evidence about who you have been.

And your future may depend on something that has never happened to you before. Something as unexpected as your friend saying:

"I'm divorcing Bob."

That creates a strange paradox:

The better a machine becomes at predicting the future we are likely to choose, the easier it may be to miss the future we would never have predicted.

And that future lives in a peculiar place.

It's a place called OUTSIDE.

Outside the highest probability, the obvious recommendation, what the machine already knows about you, and sometimes, outside everything that initially appears relevant at all.

In other words, it lives in places your personal agent may conclude are simply filled with noise.

And noise is the natural enemy of an optimizing system.

It is something to be filtered, reduced, or eliminated so that your life can be organized more efficiently around what the system can already infer about you. But think about what may be hiding there:

The improbable. The unnecessary. The inefficient. The apparently irrelevant. The accidental. The strange.

Take the time for just a moment to think about your own background, and you are likely to find something surprising in common with most successful people…those places 'outside the highest probability' are precisely the places where the turning points occurred in their lives.

The unexpected conversation. The unlikely person. The book they almost didn't read. The job they never intended to take. The idea that made no sense at first. The wrong turn that somehow became the right one.

These are the places where novelty enters our lives. Where growth begins. Where opportunity appears. And sometimes, where an entirely different future first becomes possible.

Steinbeck Understood Something About This

Near the end of John Steinbeck's East of Eden, seventeen-year-old Abra is talking to Cal about growing up. It's 1918, the world is at war, and they are living during uncertain times. For many today, that feels uncomfortably familiar.

As children, she tells him, they had lived within comfortable, predictable stories created for them. But eventually, the story was no longer enough. Reality had become larger than the story.

Her former boyfriend, Aron, she realized, could not make that transition. He needed life to conform to the story that had already been constructed. It was safe. Predictable. He could not tolerate another ending.

Abra wanted something different. She didn't need or want predictability…to know how the story would come out.

She just wanted to be there while it was going on.

There is something remarkably modern in that century-old story.

An AI agent learns your story. Your history. Your preferences. Your habits. Your relationships. Your purchases. Your ambitions. The decisions you have made before.

And then, quite reasonably, it uses that story to predict what you are likely to want next. The better your agent becomes, the more coherent the story becomes.

And perhaps the harder it becomes for something outside the story to enter your future.

That may be one of the subtler risks of extreme personalization. Not that the agent misunderstands who you are. Instead, it understands who you have been so well that it keeps showing you who you are likely to remain…and hides the alternative that reality can offer.

Growing up…personally, professionally, intellectually…has always required something else. Leaving part of the past behind. A willingness to let the story change. To encounter something that doesn't fit. To choose something your past could not have predicted.

Not always knowing how it comes out. And, as Abra decides, to be there when it happens.

Prediction has a temporal bias toward the past.

AI agents necessarily learn you from evidence created by the person you have already been. But human development partly consists of becoming someone for whom there is, by definition, no historical data yet.

Shannon Meets Simon

This is where our last Conversation Across Time, with Herbert Simon, unexpectedly meets this one.

Simon, a 1978 Nobel Prize winner, told us that a wealth of information creates a poverty of attention. There is simply too much information and too little human attention. As a result, making decisions requires a filter. Claude Shannon offers another way to look at the problem.

The predictable tells us relatively little that is new. The unexpected carries more information.

Let's put those two trailblazers in a room to discuss AI.

Simon would likely tell us that agents can help you make decisions by reducing the overwhelming amount of information.

But Shannon would whisper: because the system cannot conserve our attention without making judgments about relevance, be careful what you call noise.

And since Shannon was an engineer, he would likely point out that relevance requires an analytic model of what matters. And increasingly, he would foresee that in our world, those models would be personalized for us.

Which means the machine will become extraordinarily good at identifying the signal for the person it believes we are…and the rest will be treated as noise.

That may be exactly what you think you want. Until perhaps someone like me tells you:

An option capable of changing who you are arrived yesterday…but your agent thought it looked like noise.

Now Let's Turn Our Attention to Business

So far, this has been about individuals. But a business is not really a building or a balance sheet. It's a living system, run by people, making thousands of small decisions about what to say, sell, and pursue next. Increasingly, those decisions run through the same kind of predictive machine we've just described…except now it's trained not on one person's history, but on aggregated customer searches, questions, and behavior.

Ask the machine what customers want, and it will answer accurately: more of what they've already searched for, asked about, and bought. Ask it how to be found by AI search and answer engines, and it will answer accurately again: structure your content the way the highest-cited content is already structured.

Do this long enough, at scale, across an entire industry, and something Shannon would recognize starts to happen.

The channel optimizes itself into the silence of monotony.

Research on AI-driven hiring has already given this a name: algorithmic monoculture…the tendency of firms that lean on similar predictive models, trained on similar data, to converge on the same decisions, the same criteria, the same blind spots. It shows up in who gets hired. And there's no reason to think it stops there.

The same dynamic is now visible in how companies write. Brands optimizing for visibility in AI-generated answers are converging on the same structure…a direct answer in the first sentences, the same "TLDR-first" format, because that's what current AI search systems reward. One recent analysis found that only about 30 percent of brands stay visible from one AI-generated answer to the next, and just 20 percent remain visible across five consecutive queries…a volatility that pushes companies toward whatever format seems to survive, rather than whatever is actually distinctive.

Separately, the overwhelming majority of AI citations now trace back to earned media rather than a company's own words, which is quietly pushing marketing departments toward the same handful of outlets and the same pitch, chasing the same mentions.

None of this is a conspiracy. Every individual decision is defensible, even smart. Match the format that performs. Say what customers are already asking. Get covered where the citations come from. It's Simon's satisficing, applied at the level of the organization: good enough, informed by what's already worked for companies like this one.

But a business built this way is doing to itself exactly what the personalized agent does to an individual when it reasons entirely from precedent:

It becomes less capable of imagining a new and potentially successful strategy for which no precedent yet exists.

When agentic systems tell us what customers want, we make more of what they tell us customers want.

When they tell us what content performs, we create more of what already performs.

If they tell us that a haunting image of a painted face attracts attention, we'll probably create more of those too. After all, you probably paused to look at that image before reading this article. If the agent noticed, just imagine what happens next.

All of this seems perfectly rational…until we suddenly realize that we are reshaping ourselves to be rewarded by the greatest prediction machines in history.

And there is a problem with that.

I call it Predictive Convergence, the condition that emerges when a company's strategy and voice are increasingly shaped both by what predictive systems say customers want, and for what those same systems are likely to reward.

Eventually, the two loops begin to close on each other, and everyone gets better at moving toward the same place.

Every decision leading there may appear entirely rational. Match the format that performs. Follow the signals customers are already sending. Do more of what the data says is working.

But competitors are doing the same thing…using similar agentic systems, trained on similar industrial histories, responding to similar customer signals.

And then something unexpected happens.

The better everyone becomes at predicting what will work, the more alike everyone begins to look.

The strange part is that this can happen to a company that is winning by every visible metric. Rising citation counts. Rising qualified traffic. Every quarter, validating the loop that produced it.

But the cost is invisible:

The strategy that might have emerged if nobody had gone looking for what was already popular first.

Think for a moment about some of the ideas that changed entire categories.

  • A talking gecko selling automobile insurance.
  • A woman with a sledgehammer running into a room of gray conformists to introduce the Apple Macintosh.
  • A pink mechanical rabbit beating a drum to sell batteries.
  • Volkswagen telling Americans accustomed to enormous automobiles to Think Small.

Today, these ideas would certainly be part of a final consideration set because they are already in every AI agent's historical database.

But imagine evaluating them before they happened.

What existing data would have predicted them? What successful precedent would have justified them? What model of existing consumer behavior would have ranked them as the highest-probability answer?

That is precisely the point.

Breakthrough ideas often look obvious only after someone has had the courage to do the improbable thing first.

And that is the danger hidden inside Predictive Convergence.

Courage to choose never becomes an issue because those options never make it into the consideration set.

Perhaps We All Need Some Noise

So, perhaps the next challenge for truly intelligent agents will be different…eventually, they may need to understand not only what to remove, but when not to remove it.

Maybe we should deliberately ask them:

  • Show me something outside my normal interests.
  • Introduce me to someone you would ordinarily consider irrelevant.
  • Give me an option that doesn't fit my history.
  • Show me the low-probability possibility.
  • Tell me what you almost discarded as noise.

Perhaps the most sophisticated personal agents won't simply know us extraordinarily well. They will know when to surprise us…just as exciting and successful lives always have.

The Shannon Echo

That may leave us with a practical habit suggested by another thinker who never encountered the world we're now building.

When the machine gives you exactly what you expected, occasionally ask:

  • What surprised you?
  • What did you encounter that didn't fit?
  • What seemed irrelevant?
  • What had a low probability but potentially large consequences?
  • What did you classify as noise?

And occasionally:

Show me something you don't think I will like.

Most of the time, Shannon's prediction would probably win. It will be just noise. Discard it. Move on. But every once in a while, something else may happen.

The machine will show you the equivalent of those three unexpected words in an otherwise predictable conversation. And the world you thought you understood will explode into unexpected opportunities.

For more than seventy-five years, we have been teaching machines to reduce uncertainty. Predictive Convergence in the Agentic Age may require us to learn something more difficult:

That uncertainty is not just a container filled with noise but often a necessary stage of creation, and the most important signals in our lives and in our businesses may first arrive cloaked in that uncertainty.

What Inspired This Conversation

This Conversation draws on Claude Shannon's 1948 paper "A Mathematical Theory of Communication," John Steinbeck's East of Eden, and current research into AI-driven business decision-making: work on algorithmic monoculture in automated hiring, and 2026 findings on brand visibility volatility and earned-media dependence in generative-engine-optimized content.
