Sun Tzu's Art of War, Part Two

What battle are you trying to win that you no longer need to fight?

By J. Kimball

The Battle Sun Tzu Never Wanted to Fight

Before We Continue, Let's Get to Know Sun… If We Ever Can.

Perhaps appropriately for a man who taught that appearances can be manipulated and intentions concealed, history has never completely revealed the man behind The Art of War.

The mystery begins with his name.

We call him Sun Tzu. But Tzu was not really his name. It was an honorific, roughly meaning "Master." His traditional personal name was Sun Wu.

And the unknowns continue from there. Almost everything we know about his life comes from accounts written centuries after he was supposed to have lived.

Tradition gives us only fragments.

It tells us that Sun came from the state of Qi, in what is now northeastern China, and eventually built his military reputation hundreds of miles away in the southern kingdom of Wu.

What we do know is that the world in which he was trying to build that reputation was itself changing dramatically — in ways that feel remarkably familiar to professionals living through the waning years of our own industrial era.

It was China's Spring and Autumn period. The old political order was weakening. Rival states were competing for territory, influence, and survival. Warfare was changing with them.

Older conventions about how battles were supposed to be fought were giving way to larger armies, longer campaigns, and commanders increasingly willing to abandon familiar rules when those rules no longer worked.

Put into today's language, Sun was trying to build a career in an industry being disrupted.

And like most of us, he didn't command the largest army. He didn't rule his own kingdom. He didn't possess overwhelming resources.

But he had something potentially more valuable in a changing world.

He understood that when the environment changes, continuing to play by yesterday's rules can become more dangerous than the change itself.

Somehow, however, he still had to convince the people who did control armies and kingdoms that his way of thinking was worth listening to.

That part of Sun's story will become important later.

Because before King Helü of Wu was willing to trust this relatively unknown strategist with an army, tradition says he demanded something every professional still recognizes today.

Proof of his credibility.

And the test he supposedly gave Sun became one of the strangest job interviews in history.

We'll come back to that in the next part of our conversation.

But for now, let's move on to the question that Sun has for us — about the cost of winning.

Winning Is Expensive

Most people assume The Art of War is a book about winning battles.

In many ways, it's about something harder:

How to accomplish what you need to accomplish without fighting the battle at all.

Sun understood something military leaders — and professionals — often resist.

Winning is expensive.

Even a victory costs soldiers, resources, exposed capabilities, new enemies, and exhaustion spent on something that may not have mattered as much as it seemed to at the time.

Which is why one of Sun Tzu's most enduring ideas wasn't about destroying the opposing army.

It was that the highest form of strategy makes the battle unnecessary. That might sound philosophical. But it wasn't.

It was intensely practical.

An army exists to accomplish an objective. If the objective can be achieved without paying the enormous cost of battle, why fight simply to prove you can win?

Twenty-five centuries later, that question is worth asking again.

What battle are you trying to win that you no longer need to fight?

The Battle We Automatically Chose

For generations, professionals competed in fairly predictable ways.

  • Know more.
  • Work harder.
  • Gain more experience.
  • Produce sharper analysis.
  • Manage larger organizations.
  • Climb the professional ladder.

That made sense because the people competing with you possessed roughly the same basic resources you did.

Another lawyer could work longer hours. Another analyst could read more reports. Another consultant could build a better presentation.

But the differences remained human-sized.

AI changes that contest.

A system can read more, search faster, draft faster, compare more alternatives, remember more, and produce more versions — without waiting for its next training program.

So, the response has been remarkably predictable — and often encouraged by employers:

Use AI to get better at doing the same things, faster.

That should sound familiar. Use it to read faster. Write faster. Analyze faster. And produce more.

In other words, accept the battle and go looking for better weapons.

Amazon did exactly this, in public, and then had to rethink it.

Earlier this year, an internal Amazon dashboard called KiroRank began ranking employees by how many AI tokens they consumed — essentially creating a leaderboard for AI usage itself.

But soon, employees began inflating their token counts on low-value tasks simply to climb the rankings, a practice that became known as "tokenmaxxing."

Amazon eventually shut the leaderboard down.

Employees were told not to use AI simply for the sake of using AI, and the company shifted toward measuring something far more meaningful: the use of AI-assisted code that actually improved shipping and solved a customer's problem.

Amazon certainly wasn't thinking about Sun Tzu when it built KiroRank.

But it had built a scoreboard for the wrong battle.

Eventually, it noticed.

Sun might ask why the rest of us who believe we are adapting to AI are waiting to do the same.

What Are You Actually Trying to Accomplish?

Imagine sitting across the table from Sun Tzu.

You explain that AI can now produce in minutes work that once took you days. And somewhat boastfully, you explain how you learned to use it too. And now you can produce the same work in hours.

Sun listens. Then perhaps he asks:

Why is producing that work the objective?

That's an uncomfortable question, because often producing the work isn't the objective.

A lawyer's objective isn't to produce legal research. It's to help someone make a sound decision, protect an interest, or prevail in a dispute.

A strategist's objective isn't to build a presentation. It's to help an organization understand a problem and choose better.

A manager's objective isn't to run meetings and coordinate calendars. It's to make sure a group of people accomplishes something that matters.

An executive's objective isn't to process information faster. It's to decide what should be done when the answer isn't obvious — and accept responsibility for what happens next.

But before AI, when that intermediate work absorbed enormous amounts of human time, the work and the objective became easy to confuse.

The research felt like the value. The analysis felt like the value. The report felt like the value. The presentation felt like the value.

AI is beginning to expose the difference. And that difference is already showing up in one of the most traditional professional businesses in the world.

In November 2025, McKinsey's UK managing partner, Michael Birshan, told reporters that roughly a quarter of the firm's global fees were already tied to outcomes rather than hours.

At the same time, McKinsey's internal AI assistant, Lilli, was dramatically reducing the time consultants needed for some of the analytical work clients had traditionally paid for by the hour.

Think about what that means.

McKinsey didn't stop doing the analytical work.

AI simply made much of it faster.

And once the work took less time, something that had always been easy to overlook became increasingly difficult to ignore: clients weren't really buying the hours. They were buying the outcome.

Other major consulting firms are confronting the same change.

Which gives us a useful distinction.

Amazon measured the activity. McKinsey began pricing the outcome.

Sun Tzu would have understood the difference immediately.

Sun Would Start With the Objective

This is where this conversation and our first conversation with Sun Tzu connect.

In our first conversation, Sun asked:

Where should you compete?

Don't attack the strongest opponent where its resources matter most.

Find different ground.

But Tzu advises that there's a more fundamental question that comes before that one:

What are you actually trying to accomplish?

Because once the objective becomes clear, the battlefield can disappear entirely.

Suppose AI can perform 80 percent of the analysis underlying your work. One immediate response is:

How do I get better at the remaining 20 percent?

Sun's question might be:

Why are you competing over analysis at all?

If what your boss or your firm's clients actually want from you is what the analysis means for their situation, then the analysis was never the destination.

It was the road.

And once you understand that, you stop competing with AI over who can travel the road faster.

You start asking where the road was supposed to take you.

The Battle Before the Battle

Sun spent enormous effort thinking about war precisely because he understood how costly it was.

Long campaigns exhausted states. Supply lines became vulnerable. Even victories could weaken the winner.

So the strategist's job wasn't simply to win the battle in front of him.

It was to shape the conditions surrounding it — alliances, intelligence, morale, terrain, timing, resources — so completely that fighting became unnecessary or, when unavoidable, the outcome was already heavily tilted in his favor.

The battle comes later. Strategy comes first.

That's why Sun's thinking still travels so well beyond warfare.

And it may be exactly where many professionals are making their mistake today.

We May Be Fighting the Wrong Opponent

Listen to how we talk about AI.

Will it replace me? Can it do my job? How do I get better than it? Which skills can't it perform? How do I make myself AI-proof?

All reasonable questions. But notice what they quietly assume.

That AI is the opponent.

Sun might reject the premise.

AI is a capability in the environment.

It's being used by your employer. Your competitors. Your clients. And you.

The objective isn't to defeat it. The objective is still whatever it always was:

  • Solve the client's problem.
  • Make the better decision.
  • Build a stronger business.
  • Recognize the opportunity.
  • Earn someone's confidence.
  • Accept responsibility for an uncertain outcome.

Once you define the objective that way, the landscape changes.

AI belongs on your side of the battlefield.

Competing against it makes about as much sense as an ancient commander trying to outperform his own cavalry.

The question isn't:

Can I outperform this capability?

It becomes:

How does this capability change the best way to accomplish my objective?

That's a very different conversation.

Try Sun's Question on Your Own Work

Here's a simple way to test this.

Think about the work that occupies most of your week.

Then ask:

What outcome is this work actually supposed to produce?

Not the deliverable. Not the activity. Not the process your organization has always used.

The outcome.

Then ask:

If I were trying to achieve that outcome for the first time today, knowing what's now possible, would I still do the work this way?

And finally:

If not, why am I still fighting this battle?

Maybe your answer to the second question is yes. That's useful to know.

But if the answer is no, something important has been revealed.

You may be spending enormous energy becoming more efficient at winning a battle that no longer needs to be fought.

And that may be the hardest kind of battle to abandon.

Because it's the one you already know how to win.

The Professional Trap Sun Would Recognize

Accomplished professionals may actually find this harder than everyone else.

Success creates investment.

Years of training. Credentials. Expertise. Reputation. Authority. And perhaps most importantly, professional identity.

We become very good at doing something the existing system values.

Then the environment changes.

Our instinct isn't to question what made us successful.

It's to defend it. Improve it. Protect it. Become even better at it.

Sun would recognize the trap.

Commanders could become masters of familiar formations, familiar rules, and familiar terrain — only to encounter an opponent who refused to play by them.

Their experience hadn't become worthless. Their attachment to the conditions under which that experience worked had become dangerous.

Which gives us another way to think about adaptation:

Your experience may remain enormously valuable. The battle in which you learned to use it may not.

You don't necessarily abandon your expertise. You just need to stop assuming that yesterday's way of applying it is the only place where it has value.

Perhaps That Was Sun's Larger Point

Sun Tzu wasn't teaching people to avoid difficult situations. He was teaching them not to confuse fighting with strategy.

Sometimes you fight, or move, or wait, or deceive. Sometimes you form an alliance, or decide to change the terrain.

And sometimes you accomplish the objective without fighting at all.

The measure was never how impressively you fought.

The measure was whether you achieved what mattered.

That may be the question Sun would leave with today's professional:

Before asking how to get better at the battle in front of you, ask whether winning it still gets you where you want to go.

Because the most expensive victory may be becoming extraordinarily good at something the changing world no longer requires you to do.
