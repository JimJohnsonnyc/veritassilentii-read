Conversation No. 11 · Part III of three · November 2026

Adam Smith and the Conductor's Premium

Smith explained what different kinds of work are worth. He left out the one person everyone in the building trusts, and he did it on purpose.

By J. Kimball

The Person Everybody Trusts

We all know at least one.

Maybe they're in your department. Maybe they work in a different part of the business entirely. Either way, they seem to know people everywhere, inside the firm and outside it, far beyond anything their job requires.

Study what they do and you'll find it isn't really about connections. Most of us have connections. Some of us have hundreds. These people are different, and what makes them different is trust.

They earned it the slow way: by taking the time to understand what other people do, what problems they face, and what matters to them. Over time they became the person others turn to when a problem crosses departmental lines, when interests collide, or when something important simply has to get done.

And here's the curious part. Their job titles are usually no different from yours or mine. Nothing on the door says they're different.

I found a few people like this in almost every company I worked with. An engineer the sales force trusted. A finance person the engineers trusted. A marketing manager trusted by first-line sales and product management alike. A plant manager both management and the union trusted.

They weren't always senior. Some barely showed up on the organization chart. But when something important had to happen, everyone knew whom to call. And when one of them left the company, something stopped working that nobody could quite explain.

I tried for years to put that role on an organization chart. I never succeeded.

It is only now, after revisiting what Adam Smith thought people were paid for, that I understand why.

Their real value lives the way music lives, in the space between the notes: between people, between jobs, between the parts of the business they bring together.

In the Agentic Age, that space is about to become enormously valuable.

The Value In Between

This isn't a new observation. Business researchers have been studying these people for more than fifty years.

At MIT in the 1970s, Thomas Allen studied how information moved through research laboratories. He found that a surprisingly small number of engineers connected their organizations to knowledge outside them. He called them gatekeepers.

Hold on to that word. We'll come back to it.

Later, the sociologist Ronald Burt studied people who connected groups that otherwise rarely spoke to one another. He called the gaps between those groups structural holes, and he found that the people who bridged them often received better evaluations, faster promotions, and greater compensation than colleagues in comparable positions.

You might reasonably ask why anyone thought this was unusual. Companies have long understood the value of relationships. Consulting firms, advertising agencies, banks, and sales organizations all employ relationship managers, and often pay them very well.

But that isn't what Allen and Burt were studying. A relationship manager has a commercial purpose written into the job: clients to retain, accounts to grow, partnerships to manage, revenue targets to hit.

Allen's gatekeepers and Burt's brokers had no such assignment. They worked beyond the formal edges of their positions, connecting people in different parts of the business whose knowledge, responsibilities, and interests didn't naturally line up. They made things happen that no single department could accomplish alone, because they understood what each side needed, why those needs sometimes conflicted, and what it would take to bring them together.

That created a problem for the industrial-age pay system. Companies pay for the responsibilities attached to a position: revenue, budgets, headcount, operating results, individual expertise. Those can be measured and rewarded.

But what about the value created between positions?

We all know the person who gets two departments working together often receives a bigger bonus, an earlier promotion, or a discretionary raise. But the organization chart has no job for that contribution, and the pay system has no line for it. The company rewards what the relationships produce without ever acknowledging the relationships as work.

There's a very human result. Because bridging two sides means stepping outside an assigned role, these people are often quietly resented by colleagues who haven't built those skills. Yet the same colleagues will admit, when pressed, that they are indispensable.

This Brings Us Back to Adam Smith

So is the value of relationships missing from Smith's map of pay?

In Part I, we reviewed Smith's five explanations for why some work pays more than others. I translated them into five zones: Burden, Expertise, Continuity, Outcome Risk, and Authority. In Part II, we looked at how AI is changing the economics of each one, and arrived at the Agentic Pay Cliff.

Now notice what those five zones have in common. Each describes a characteristic of the work, or of the person doing it: the hardship endured, the cost of acquiring expertise, the uncertainty of employment, the probability of success, the trust and responsibility attached to the job.

You may be thinking: isn't trust already on that list?

It is. Smith's fourth circumstance is the trust reposed in the worker, and the Authority zone is where I put it. He was thinking of the goldsmith, the physician, the lawyer, the people we trust with our fortune or our health. That is trust in a person's role.

What I'm describing is different. It is the trust of several parties whose interests conflict, all placed in the same person at the same moment. No license earns it. It's earned by years of being fair to both sides.

In the Industrial Age, most jobs were paid for some combination of the five zones, and for the most part that worked. But it never fully recognized these people. They made a different kind of economic contribution, always in the background, never on the books.

I came to think of it as a shadow role. For a long time I assumed Smith missed it because he was looking too narrowly, at the workshops he studied and the people who did the work.

I no longer think that.

Smith didn't miss the relationship role. He knew it was there. He was trying to get rid of it.

What Smith Left Out on Purpose

For centuries before Smith, business was done between people who knew each other. A merchant in one port trusted a merchant in another because they shared a name, a church, or a cousin, and because everyone knew who had cheated whom. Credit across Europe ran on reputation as much as on collateral. Those relationships were how commerce got coordinated, and the roles inside a business existed to carry out what the relationships had already agreed.

Smith looked at that world and saw its cost. Merchants had the ear of ministers. Monopolies and chartered companies were built on relationships, and so were guild privileges.

His answer was the invisible hand, a better alternative to the visible handshake. He even left a warning about the handshake in The Wealth of Nations: people of the same trade seldom meet together, even for merriment, without the conversation ending in a conspiracy against the public. And he wrote the most famous sentence in the book: it is not from the benevolence of the butcher that we expect our dinner.

His point was that impersonal exchange could do what personal trust had always done, and do it better, because a stranger has no favorites.

He even seems to have believed commerce would produce the trust it replaced. In his Glasgow lectures, he observed that wherever commerce takes hold, probity and punctuality follow it. A dealer is afraid of losing his character.

Read that way, his chapter on wages is a list of what a stranger will pay for. It's what is left to price once you take the relationships out of the wage and let the market set the rate. That's why Smith's five zones describe what a person brings, and never whom a person connects.

The professional who brings people and departments together isn't something Smith overlooked. It's the thing The Wealth of Nations was designed to make unnecessary.

His own Glasgow had a name and a price for that role. The tobacco merchants who made the city rich sent men across the Atlantic to buy the crop at the source, on credit, from stores along the rivers of the Chesapeake. They were called factors.

The grower had to trust the factor, because he graded the crop and secured the debt. The merchant had to trust him, because he was committing money an ocean away from anyone who could check. The factor was a trusted bridge between two parties who both depended entirely on his judgment, and he was paid for it, by commission on the trade.

The bridge was visible then because the modern firm hadn't swallowed it yet. Once it did, the factor became a corporate position, a regional manager, say, with a title inside the company and pay attached to the position. The trusted bridge came along for free.

The historian Alfred Chandler called the industrial-age management system the visible hand. What it did, in effect, was replace personal trust with institutional trust: the contract, the audit, the brand, the credential, the job description, the organization chart. Sociologists such as Lynne Zucker have described the shift as trust in persons giving way to trust in systems.

But some part of personal trust, something deeply human, the systems could never absorb. For two centuries it was carried along as a shadow role inside management structures built to make it unnecessary, doing work those structures couldn't do.

Now agentic systems are taking over more of the management of the business itself, and the roles built on the five zones are being hollowed out. And the one thing Smith wanted to replace, the one thing the industrial age then spent two hundred and fifty years engineering out, is stepping out of the shadows: the person several parties, with different interests, all trust.

I'm beginning to suspect the industrial age may have been a brief exception to patterns that prevailed for centuries, and that those patterns are returning, in a different form.

A Word About Relationships

Two cautions before we go on.

First, the world Smith attacked deserved it. Relationship economics was closed, kin-bound, and often corrupt. An economy where pay follows who trusts you has a who-you-know problem built in. The Conductor, whom you're about to meet, is my name for the good version of that timeless role. The bad version is cronyism with agents, and the difference between them is the subject of the rest of this essay.

Second, Smith did not ignore relationships. He put them in a different book. The Theory of Moral Sentiments, which he wrote first and revised until the year he died, is about sympathy, trust, and the impartial spectator who judges whether we deserve the confidence others place in us.

Scholars spent a century puzzling over how one man wrote both books. They even gave it a name: the Adam Smith Problem. One answer is that he thought markets should handle commerce and sympathy should handle everything else, and that the two would never need to meet.

The Agentic Age is where they meet. The Conductor is a figure from Moral Sentiments who has just been handed The Wealth of Nations to manage.

The Conductor

At the end of Part II, I asked you to imagine an orchestra: thousands of accomplished musicians, every instrument available, every piece of music ever written, and nobody at the front.

AI is giving businesses something like that orchestra, an extraordinary collection of capabilities on demand. So what becomes valuable when capabilities are no longer scarce?

The person who knows what to do with them.

I call that person the Conductor. I don't mean someone who knows how to run the software. That's a useful skill, and in Part II I called the person who has it the AI Operator. But operating a machine and directing the value it creates are different jobs.

Think about what a conductor actually does.

First, the conductor decides what's worth playing. In business, that means knowing which problem deserves attention and what outcome would make solving it worthwhile.

Second, the conductor can read the score. Not well enough to play every instrument, but well enough to know when something is wrong. You can't conduct music you can't read, and you can't direct an agent in a domain you don't understand. Expertise doesn't stop mattering. It stops being what you're paid for and becomes what qualifies you to be paid.

Third, the conductor hears the whole performance. A musician can play every note correctly and still miss what the music requires. An AI system can produce a technically impressive answer to the wrong business question.

Finally, the conductor answers for the result. When a performance fails, the audience doesn't blame the third violin. It looks at the person holding the baton. When the agent decides and the outcome goes wrong, the liability, in Jamie Dimon's phrase from Part II, transfers to the nearest human who agreed to it.

Now go back to the beginning of this essay, to the people who seem to know everyone and who earned trust by understanding what each group does, the problems it faces, and what matters to it. They were conducting long before anyone called it that.

The Conductor isn't a new kind of person the Agentic Age has to invent. It's the shadow role, finally visible. And it's evolving, because nearly everything else the organization needs is getting cheaper. Agents can now supply what the five zones priced: the hours, the knowledge, the process, the retrievable expertise.

What can't be absorbed into an AI system is the person several groups with different interests all believe, trust, and follow.

Remember Allen's gatekeepers? Software is now building gates of its own, what I've called the Agentic Gate, the layer of AI that decides what and who gets through. But a software gate filters. It doesn't earn trust. The human gatekeeper held that position because people believed in them, and that is the part a filter cannot supply.

The Conductor's Premium

Smith's five zones explain much of what professionals have traditionally been paid for. But the Agentic Age is revealing a sixth source of value, one that belongs to the individual but is created between people.

I call it the Conductor's Premium.

A Conductor can bring together and direct the capabilities represented by Smith's other five zones toward an outcome that matters, and, just as important, the people involved trust them to do it.

That draws on qualities that once commanded premiums in their own right. Expertise, which intelligent systems can now supply for many tasks. Authority, which once came with a position but increasingly has to be demonstrated through consequential decisions. And judgment and trust, which no title can guarantee.

But a Conductor does something more. They bring those capabilities together, often across departments, organizations, and competing interests, to accomplish something none could achieve alone.

In the Agentic Age, the leverage of that ability could become extraordinary.

Consider someone who once needed a department of twenty people to accomplish an important objective. That same person may soon direct a collection of intelligent agents toward a comparable result. If they have the authority to decide what should be done, the judgment to know whether it was done right, and the trust of the people who depend on the outcome, their value is multiplied. Not because they work harder. Because they can direct more capability.

There is an echo of this in the merchants and factors of Smith's Glasgow. Their contribution wasn't only the work they did themselves. It was their ability to connect buyers, sellers, capital, and opportunities that might otherwise never have met.

But one qualification is essential to understanding this sixth zone.

Creating more value doesn't mean you'll capture it.

A Conductor needs enough recognized authority, independence, or bargaining power to claim a meaningful share of the additional value created. Without it, the premium goes to the employer or the platform owner, and the Conductor becomes the shadow role again: paid for an industrial-age position while much of the value created between positions is captured by someone else.

The Uncomfortable Question

Smith's five zones describe circumstances that influence the pay for work. The Conductor's Premium describes a capability: combining and directing work, and capturing part of the additional value that results.

Before we go on, there's an uncomfortable question to answer. It requires being objective about your own value.

First, imagine an intelligent agent can do the work you do now. Another agent, working for the person who receives that work, can evaluate the results.

What value do you still bring to the transaction?

Now turn it around. Imagine you're the person depending on that work. The outcome is critically important. The agents can perform the tasks, but you aren't prepared to trust their judgment or accept the consequences of a mistake.

What would you need from a person before you were willing to trust the outcome?

Now ask one more question.

Are you that person?

Your answers may tell you more about your future pay and job security than your current title ever could.

Where You Stand on the Pyramid

Not everyone will become a Conductor. Nor does everyone need to.

The Agentic Age will still need people who operate systems, manage processes, solve problems, and deliver results. Many will earn good incomes doing it.

But the professional pyramid is changing. Much of the work at its base and through its middle, researching, analyzing, drafting, reporting, coordinating, is increasingly performed by intelligent agents. As we saw in Part II, the pay attached to that work may eventually meet the Agentic Pay Cliff.

Below the cliff will be the AI Operators. They'll direct systems, monitor results, and keep work moving. For many professionals these will be valuable careers, at least through the early stages of the transition.

Above the cliff will be people with the authority, judgment, and trust to direct increasingly consequential outcomes. Some will command the Conductor's Premium.

But that raises a problem that may be more serious than the loss of individual jobs.

Where will the next generation of Conductors come from?

The Ladder We Are Dismantling

Think about how professionals have always learned their work.

Junior lawyers researched cases. Young engineers worked alongside experienced ones. Analysts prepared reports for senior managers. Salespeople learned from difficult customers. Much of that work was repetitive. Some of it was inefficient.

But it did something important. It developed the judgment and experience that eventually earned other people's trust.

The professional ladder was never just a pay system. It was a training system.

And we're dismantling it.

AI can now do much of the routine work that once gave young professionals their experience. From an employer's view, the economics are compelling. Why pay someone three days to prepare a report an intelligent system can produce in minutes?

Because those three days were never just about the report. They were about developing the person who would eventually know whether the report was right.

If that sounds familiar, it's the same room we visited with Keats and Wallas in Conversation No. 6: the associate who is handed an AI-generated memo instead of a blank page and a deadline, and never builds the judgment that comes from sitting with an unresolved problem. A Conductor's judgment is built in that room.

The old ladder was far from perfect. But it produced experienced people at a scale the economy could support. We'll need another way.

Medicine has residencies. Aviation requires supervised flying hours. Both recognize that knowledge alone doesn't qualify anyone to accept responsibility for consequential decisions. Other professions will have to find their own solutions.

The AI Operator could become the first rung of a new ladder, but only if employers deliberately create opportunities to develop judgment, responsibility, and relationships across organizational boundaries. If they don't, ambitious professionals will have to seek those opportunities themselves.

Find problems that cross departments. Work with people whose interests differ from yours. Take responsibility for outcomes. Earn the trust of people who don't necessarily trust one another.

That's how Conductors develop.

There's an irony here. We may be building an economy with extraordinary productive capability while dismantling the system that produces people qualified to direct it. If that happens, the Conductor's Premium could become even more valuable, precisely because too few people are able to earn it.

The Smith Echo

So where do three conversations with Adam Smith leave us?

Smith identified five reasons different kinds of work command different rewards. The Industrial Age built an enormous professional class around those differences.

AI is now changing their economics. Expertise is getting easier to obtain. Many tasks need fewer people. Some positions will lose economic value long before their titles or salaries change.

That's the Agentic Pay Cliff.

But economic value doesn't simply disappear. Much of it moves. And one place it may be gathering is around people who can bring together expertise, resources, and intelligent systems to accomplish something that matters. People with the judgment to know what should be done, the authority to make consequential decisions, and the trust of those who depend on the outcome.

We've always needed these people. What changes in the Agentic Age is how much capability they can direct, and with it, the economic value they can create.

The Industrial Age paid people for the expertise they possessed and the positions they occupied. The Agentic Age may increasingly pay for what people can bring together, what they can direct, and what others trust them to deliver.

Think back to the person I described at the beginning of this essay, the one everyone turns to when something important needs to happen. That person may have had an ordinary title. But the value they created was anything but ordinary.

The Agentic Age may finally reveal how much that capability is worth.

Which leaves one last question.

If the people who depend on your work had to decide whom to trust with the baton, would they hand it to you?

This concludes the three-part conversation with Adam Smith.

What Inspired This Conversation

This Conversation draws on Adam Smith's The Wealth of Nations (1776), including Book I, Chapter 2 (the butcher, the brewer and the baker) and Book I, Chapter 10 (the circumstances that explain differences in wages, and the remark on tradesmen meeting together); Smith's The Theory of Moral Sentiments (1759; sixth edition 1790); and the student notes of his Lectures on Jurisprudence (1762–1763).
